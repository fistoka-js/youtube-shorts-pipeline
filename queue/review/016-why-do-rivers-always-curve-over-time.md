## Final script
```text
Picture a river carving through a flat plain — perfectly straight at first, calm and quiet. Now watch what happens over time. The moment water hits any tiny bump or bend, something unstoppable begins. Water on the outside of that curve speeds up and starts eating into the bank. Meanwhile, the slower water on the inside drops its sediment, building up new land. The curve grows. Then that fast-moving water swings across the channel and carves the next curve downstream. And the next. Each bend feeds the one after it, a chain reaction locked in motion. Over time, the curves grow wilder and wilder until a loop bends so far it meets itself. The river jumps to the shorter path, and the old loop gets cut off — left behind as a crescent-shaped lake called an oxbow. The river you saw at the beginning? It's completely transformed. This is why no natural river stays straight for long. Follow for more science.
```

## Raw script as the model first returned it (before cleaning)
```text
Picture a perfectly straight river cutting through a flat valley. Within a few thousand years, it's gone — replaced by wild, looping curves snaking across the landscape. How does that happen?

It starts with the tiniest wobble. Water hits one bank slightly harder, eroding it. That pushes flow toward the opposite bank. Now the river has a curve. And here's where it gets wild.

On the outside of every bend, water moves faster — like a car on a highway taking the outer lane. That speed carves the bank deeper. On the inside, water slows down, dropping its sediment, building new land. The curve gets more curved. Which makes water move faster on the outside. Which carves even more. It's a feedback loop that never stops — until the river loops so far it crashes into itself, cuts a shortcut, and leaves behind a stranded crescent of water called an oxbow lake.

That perfectly straight river from the start? Physics wouldn't allow it to stay that way. Rivers don't curve by accident. They curve because that's exactly what water does when it's free. Follow for more science.
```
